# Better labels: put station truth into the loss

Literature review of 30 October 2026, made after the station check
([`station_validation.md`](station_validation.md)). The question: how to keep a U-Net from learning the errors of
the BTD rule labels. It is the starting point for the next step, not a decision. "The notes" below are the research
notes behind this review; they are not kept in the repository. Numbers marked as measured in this project come from
`station_validation.md`.

A U-Net trained only on the BTD rule's labels will mostly copy the rule's errors. Those errors are systematic, not random. No published study shows a network trained on a fog rule's output beating that rule against stations. The fix is to make the 58,600 station cases do the corrective work: as a point loss at station pixels, as a calibration of how much to trust each rule label, and as training data for a station-trained "teacher" model. Robust loss functions and label-correction tricks help little against noise this structured. Relabelling cheaply where the rule is known to be blind helps more. That means fog under high cloud, the ±0.5 K margin, and the smoothness test that rejects 60 % of missed visible fog. The best published reference is Jahani et al. 2025. Their XGBoost trained on METAR reached CSI 0.60 against the project rule's 0.42, but that score is daytime-validated. On unseen stations it only matched the physical scheme. So a realistic goal is a clear, honestly measured CSI gain over 0.42 on visible fog at held-out stations and nights. Matching 0.60 is a stretch. The plan below runs from hours-long fixes to a two-week core step. All tuning happens inside the 2023-24 season. The 2024-25 season is opened once, at the end.

## Rule labels carry systematic errors that a network learns first

The evidence that deep networks tolerate label noise is about *random* noise. Rolnick et al. showed CNNs survive large amounts of uniformly random label noise, and that result does not cover structured noise ([arXiv 1705.10694](https://arxiv.org/abs/1705.10694)). In remote sensing, structured errors hurt most. With aerial images, **5 % wrong pixels already cost at least 3 % accuracy**. Shifted labels did the most harm, and random noise the least ([Maiti et al. 2022](https://isprs-annals.copernicus.org/articles/V-2-2022/275/2022/)). The BTD rule's errors are of the structured kind. It misses fog whose BTD looks like clear ground. It rejects patchy fog through the smoothness test. It calls every pixel under high cloud "no fog". And its false fog drifts north with viewing angle (all measured in this project).

Segmentation networks first fit the easy, consistent labels and memorize wrong ones later. ADELE exploits this, but its authors warn that **"highly structured noise" can prevent early learning**, and that poor initial labels can give marginal or negative gains ([arXiv 2110.03740](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2110.03740)). A bias that is consistent over a whole region is exactly what a network finds easy. Noisy-label training also needs scale. OSM-label segmentation worked "only with substantially larger training sets" ([Kaiser et al. 2017](https://arxiv.org/abs/1707.06879)). This project has about 300 training scenes. The one counter-example is an EGU21 abstract. There, a U-Net trained on Fmask cloud labels plus self-training "can outperform its teacher", but no numbers were published ([EGU21-15914](https://meetingorganizer.copernicus.org/EGU21/EGU21-15914.html)).

The notes found **no controlled study comparing a net trained on rule labels with the rule itself against independent truth**. That is the core evidence gap. The safe working assumption follows from it. A U-Net fitted to the rule will reproduce the rule, perhaps with smoother edges. It can only move past the rule if new information enters the loss. The only such information this project has is the station network.

## Physics-based label fixes are cheap, but few are proven at night

The classical night scheme is Cermak & Bendix (2007). It uses the 10.8 − 3.9 µm BTD with **thresholds derived per satellite viewing-angle bin** from the scene histogram, because CO2 absorption at 3.9 µm depends on path length ([NASA abstract](https://modis.gsfc.nasa.gov/sci_team/pubs/abstract_new.php?id=02133)). The notes recommend this adaptive threshold as the top label upgrade. **This project has already tested it, and it did not help on its own.** At station pixels, a threshold rising 0.10 K per degree of viewing angle scored HSS 0.317. A clear-ground peak per latitude band plus an offset scored 0.278. One fixed threshold scored 0.351. Both variants evened out false fog between south and north, but lost much northern fog. The reason is visible in the project data. Clear-ground BTD rises ~0.9-1.0 K from south to north, but fog BTD rises only ~0.5 K. A threshold that tracks clear ground overshoots fog in the north. The open-source fogpy night algorithm is no better here. It bins by **solar** zenith angle, fits a linear threshold, and uses no spatial, height or surface tests ([fogpy algorithms.py](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/pytroll/fogpy/master/fogpy/algorithms.py)). No station validation of SOFOS night or fogpy night was found.

Three cheaper rule fixes target errors that this project has measured. First, **fog under high cloud should not be labelled 0**. The rule cannot see there, so "0" means "unknown", not "clear". This affects 51 % of FLS reports. Second, the margin around 4 K is wider than the current ±0.5 K ignore band suggests. FLS becomes more likely than not only ~0.5 K above the threshold, so labels just above 4 K are close to coin flips. Third, **60 % of missed visible FLS fail the 3x3 smoothness test**. That test removes real fog, not only noise. Jahani et al. treated the obscured case explicitly. Station FLS under other cloud became a "multi-layer" case and was excluded or relabelled. The authors called this screening "essential for making the ground observations compatible with what is seen from space" ([Jahani et al. 2025](https://amt.copernicus.org/articles/18/1927/2025/amt-18-1927-2025.pdf)).

Independent physical tests exist but cost more. SOFOS estimates cloud-top height from cloud-entity margins against a DEM, or from a lapse rate. This uses only IR108 and a DEM, so it works at night, and fogpy implements it as `LowCloudHeightAlgorithm` ([fogpy algorithms.py](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/pytroll/fogpy/master/fogpy/algorithms.py)). CM SAF CLAAS-3 is the only related product found that covers 2023-25. It is extended as an ICDR with ~10-day latency at 15-min native resolution ([CM SAF](https://cmsaf.eu/EN/Highlights/Dokumente/News_35.html)). It gives cloud mask, phase and cloud-top height day and night. Its low-cloud validation is "more mixed", and it has no fog product ([Benas et al. 2023](https://essd.copernicus.org/articles/15/5153/2023/)). NWC SAF Cloud Type has "very low" and "low" classes. But it must be run locally under licence with NWP input. It sometimes misses low cloud at night over land, and it calls very low cloud mid-level under strong inversions ([EUMETSAT CWG](https://cwg.eumetsat.int/?p=1026)). The GOES-R FLS product swaps the plain BTD for a naive Bayes fusion of satellite, NWP humidity and surface data. Under high cloud it "will rely on model data" ([CIMSS Fused Fog](https://fusedfog.ssec.wisc.edu/page/52/)). That is the only operational route to FLS under high cloud. But it makes the labels NWP-driven, and an IR-only U-Net cannot reproduce them. None of these products is fog truth. Each mainly helps as a feature or a cross-check.

## Station truth is the only signal that can lift POD above 0.50

The European methods that beat a single threshold all train on stations. Egli et al. (2018) trained a random forest per SEVIRI scene on METAR and SYNOP cloud base, with terrain as a predictor. They report **HSS 0.58**, and say the method works at sun elevations below 0° ([MDPI](https://www.mdpi.com/2072-4292/10/4/628)). Their earlier SOFOS-based climatology scored **HSS 0.45** against METAR ([KIT record](https://publikationen.bibliothek.kit.edu/1000067696)). The project rule scores HSS 0.37, though the station sets and definitions differ. Jahani et al. (2025) trained XGBoost on 1.17 M METAR points, 3.8 % of them FLS. They used four IR channels, three BTDs and the 3x3 standard deviation of each. On the training set they got **POD 0.80, FAR 0.30, CSI 0.60**, and POD 0.82, FAR 0.31, CSI 0.60 on unseen winters ([Jahani et al. 2025](https://amt.copernicus.org/articles/18/1927/2025/)).

Three caveats limit that benchmark. Its labels were cleaned with daytime SOFOS tests, so **the reported skill is daytime only**. Night skill was never checked against stations. On unseen stations, the ML model only **equalled the SOFOS threshold scheme on CSI**. And at rare-fog stations its POD (0.70) was below SOFOS (0.76) ([AMT 2025](https://amt.copernicus.org/articles/18/1927/2025/)). Station training helps, but the gain can shrink at new locations. The project's "not under high cloud" scores fit this benchmark well, because Jahani also removed multi-layer cases. Jahani also left out 3.9 µm to share one model between day and night. At night, 3.9 µm is the key fog channel, so this project should keep it.

Two further ideas from Jahani transfer directly. One is a **temporal confidence filter**. A station label counts only when the reports before and after it agree, within one hour. The other is 3x3 standard deviation features, which handle the gap between a point and a pixel of several km ([AMT 2025](https://amt.copernicus.org/articles/18/1927/2025/)). The 58,600 training cases are about 20 times fewer than Jahani's. That is enough for a tabular model. For a U-Net they amount to only ~200 labelled pixels per scene.

Masked point losses on sparse labels are established outside fog. A frost-forecasting segmentation model applies its loss only at observatory pixels. It adds a **surrogate loss that optimizes CSI** directly ([SKKU record](https://scholarx.skku.edu/item/4fd38fcd-4155-4a81-a454-019570d71a0b)). Partial cross-entropy plus a dense CRF-like term on unlabelled pixels gained **1.6 to 5.1 % mean F1** in point-supervised remote-sensing segmentation ([PolyU / IGARSS 2024](https://research.polyu.edu.hk/en/publications/an-end-to-end-point-supervised-method-for-semantic-segmentation-o/)). Mixing dense weak labels with sparse accurate ones is also established. LESS uses a separate loss term for each supervision type ([arXiv 2210.08064](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2210.08064)). Pretraining on noisy maps and then fine-tuning works well for remote-sensing segmentation ([Liu et al. 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.16164)). Two fog studies densify station labels. One GK-2A study kept only operational-product fog confirmed by visibility < 1 km ([EGU26-9336](https://meetingorganizer.copernicus.org/EGU26/EGU26-9336.html)). A UAE night SEVIRI study found that fusing METAR labels with k-means cluster labels worked best. It reports POD ≈ 0.73 and FAR ≈ 0.11 at Abu Dhabi, from the abstract only ([ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S2666017225001038)). No published work combines a physical-rule mask with station points in one loss for satellite fog. The design has to be built from these parts.

## Robust losses and label correction add little against structured noise

Robust losses such as SCE, GCE and Active-Passive losses were benchmarked on image classification with synthetic noise ([SCE](https://arxiv.org/abs/1908.06112); [APL](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2006.13554)). Their guarantees assume noise that is symmetric within a class. They cannot know that northern clear ground or patchy fog is suspect. In the one segmentation test found, **GCE (43.4 mIoU) and SCE (41.2) both fell below plain cross-entropy (51.0)**, and the authors chose CE with label smoothing ([arXiv 2603.00372](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2603.00372)). Co-teaching swaps small-loss samples between two networks ([arXiv 1804.06872](https://arxiv.org/abs/1804.06872)). It assumes wrong labels have large loss. Systematic regional errors are learned early and get small loss, so it suits this case poorly. Snorkel-style label models learn the accuracies of several labelling functions without supervision ([Ratner et al.](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1711.10160)). No remote-sensing segmentation use was found, and it is overkill for one rule with a few tests.

The useful core of these ideas is to model the noise with the stations. A noise transition matrix ([Patrini et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/1609.03683)) has only two free numbers per condition for a binary task. Those numbers are the chance of false fog and the chance of missed fog. The 58,600 cases can estimate them per latitude band, per margin bin and per high-cloud flag. A simpler version is a logistic fit of station truth on the rule's BTD margin and a few context variables. Its output becomes the soft dense target. This turns the station network into a calibration of the label noise with few parameters, which suits ~300 scenes. Cheap regularizers are still worth a run. Label smoothing of 0.05-0.1 and soft bootstrapping, where the target is partly the model's own prediction ([Reed et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/1412.6596)), are segmentation-compatible. Test GCE or SCE only as a one-line ablation.

## A seven-step plan that keeps the test season sealed

Freeze the evaluation before changing anything. The 2023-24 season has ~75 sampled nights. Hold out about 15 of them in contiguous blocks of several nights, because fog episodes last days and neighbouring nights are not independent. Also hold out about 20 % of stations in contiguous spatial blocks. Neighbouring stations, and stations with valley fog, would otherwise leak. Jahani's split into unseen winters, unseen stations and both shows that results differ by split ([AMT 2025](https://amt.copernicus.org/articles/18/1927/2025/)). Fit every station-based calibration and teacher on training stations and training nights only. Choose all thresholds, loss weights and early-stopping epochs on the validation block. **Never select a model by agreement with the rule labels**, because that rewards copying the rule. Score the rule and every model on the same validation station pixels. Split the scores by latitude band and by high-cloud flag. Use a paired bootstrap over nights for confidence intervals. Keep the high-cloud flag definition fixed, so that "visible FLS" means the same set of cases for every model. Open 2024-25 once, at the end. Report it on all stations and on the held-out station block separately.

| Order | Step | Size | What to do | Keep it if |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 0 | Freeze evaluation | Hours | Make the night and station split above. Score the rule on it as the bar to beat. | — |
| 1 | Stop calling hidden fog clear | Hours | Output three classes: FLS / not FLS / high cloud. Pixels the rule's high-cloud tests reject get the high-cloud class, not 0. Fallback: label them 255. | Validation CSI on visible FLS does not drop. FAR on high cloud stays low. |
| 2 | Soften the threshold | 1 day | Fit a logistic model of station FLS on BTD margin plus latitude band and IR108 (training stations only). Use it as a soft target, and widen the ignore band to where p is near 0.5. | Calibration curve is sensible on validation stations. |
| 3 | Revisit the smoothness test | 1 day | Relax the 1.5 K std limit, or turn it into a down-weight instead of a hard 0. Check the station POD/FAR trade-off. | HSS at validation stations rises. |
| 4 | Baseline U-Net | 2-3 days | Small U-Net, CE + label smoothing 0.05-0.1, ignore 255, flips and small noise only, early stopping on validation station HSS/CSI. | This is the reference. Expect it to roughly match the rule. |
| 5 | Station point loss | 1 week | Add masked CE at station pixels, weighted well above the dense term. Use temporally confident reports only, and a 3x3 neighbourhood max for patchy fog. Station FLS under high cloud gets the high-cloud class or is ignored. Run an ablation without it. | CSI CI over nights clears the rule's. North and south strata both improve. |
| 6 | Station-trained teacher | 1-2 weeks | XGBoost on station pixels: the 8 channels, BTDs, 3x3 std, viewing angle, IR108, DEM. It is both a Jahani-style benchmark and, if it beats the rule, a source of dense soft labels to replace rule labels in step 5. | Teacher beats the rule on held-out stations, not only on training stations. |
| 7 | Optional extras | 1-3 weeks each | One round of conservative self-training using the unsampled nights of 2023-24. DEM-margin cloud-top height from fogpy as an input or test. CLAAS-3 cloud-top height and phase as a cross-check. | Each passes the same station test. |

Step 6 deserves a word on why it should succeed where the adaptive threshold failed. The tested thresholds were fitted to clear ground. A station-trained model is fitted to the target itself. It can learn that the right BTD cut depends jointly on viewing angle and ground temperature, and the project has measured both effects. Jahani's own result is the caution: gains can fade at unseen stations. Step 6 counts only if it wins on the held-out block. Three things should be skipped. Do not make the threshold viewing-angle adaptive on its own (already tested). Do not adopt the CSI-optimal 3.5 K threshold, which labels half of the cloud-free northern ground as fog. And do not set up NWC SAF or an RTM clear-sky BTD for a portfolio project. FLS under high cloud stays out of scope for an IR-only network. Report all-case POD (0.22 for the rule) next to visible-case POD so the limit is plain.

## Conclusion

The question is less "which noisy-label method?" and more "where does information beyond the rule come from?" At this data size, the station network is the only source, and it should be spent in three ways. It calibrates how far each rule label can be trusted. It supervises the network directly at about 200 pixels per scene. And it judges every choice on held-out stations and nights. The rule labels are still useful, but as a dense, down-weighted prior and pretraining signal, not as the target.

For a EUMETSAT-facing portfolio, the strongest result is not a CSI number near Jahani's daytime 0.60. It is a clean ablation: rule, U-Net on rule labels, U-Net plus point loss, and teacher-distilled U-Net. All four would be scored on the same sealed stations and nights, with bootstrap intervals and north/south and high-cloud strata. That design shows, rather than claims, that the network stopped learning its label errors. It also fills a gap the notes found in the literature, which has no station-validated night-time fog segmentation for SEVIRI over Europe.
